## 2. LinkedIn post draft (900–1400 characters)

AI-assisted invention work creates a record problem before it creates a patent problem.

The weak record is not usually “we used AI.” The weak record is that, six months later, no one can show what the people contributed.

For a founder, CTO, GC, or product leader, the practical file is short:

• What technical problem did the team frame?
• Who selected the architecture, constraints, experiments, data, and tradeoffs?
• What did the AI system generate, suggest, or automate?
• Which outputs did humans reject, modify, validate, or implement?
• Where do code, model output, contractor work, and roadmap ownership sit?

That record is not a substitute for legal analysis. But it helps counsel evaluate inventorship, claim direction, trade-secret boundaries, ownership, and disclosure timing before a demo, investor conversation, customer launch, or diligence request turns into a scramble.

The opportunity is not to ban AI from R&D. It is to make the human technical judgment visible while the work is still fresh.

I’m framing this for Mountain West AI and software companies as an “AI Invention File”: a lightweight operating layer for teams that want AI speed without losing the patent and ownership story.

Educational only; not legal advice or an inventorship, patentability, or eligibility determination.

## 3. Short email / newsletter draft

**Subject:** Prompt logs are not an AI patent strategy

**Preview text:** The useful record is what the humans contributed, decided, changed, and validated.

**Body:**

AI-assisted R&D is moving faster than most invention-disclosure processes.

The issue is not simply whether a team used ChatGPT, Copilot, a model-development tool, or an internal AI system. The more useful executive question is whether the company can later show the human technical contribution behind the work.

A practical AI Invention File should be short enough to use in real product development, but disciplined enough to help counsel assess inventorship, claim direction, trade-secret boundaries, ownership, and disclosure timing.

For each important AI-assisted feature or technical advance, ask:

1. What problem did the team define?
2. Who directed architecture, constraints, data, experiments, and tradeoffs?
3. What did the AI system produce or accelerate?
4. What did humans reject, modify, test, validate, or implement?
5. Where do code, contractor work, model outputs, and roadmap ownership sit?

That is not a legal conclusion. It is a better starting record before a customer launch, investor meeting, patent filing, or diligence request.

## 9. Compliance and ethics check — claims to soften before publication

- Avoid saying that a company “must” keep an AI Invention File unless tied to a verified legal requirement; use “should consider” or “practical record.”
- Avoid any definitive statement that a specific AI-assisted invention is patentable, unpatentable, eligible, ineligible, or has correct inventorship.
- Do not imply that prompt logs alone prove or disprove human contribution.
- Do not invite confidential invention facts in LinkedIn comments, public forms, or email replies.
- Keep USPTO guidance phrasing careful: AI tools are not inventors, but human inventorship and contribution analysis are fact-specific.
- Add “educational only; not legal advice; no attorney-client relationship” near public CTAs.
