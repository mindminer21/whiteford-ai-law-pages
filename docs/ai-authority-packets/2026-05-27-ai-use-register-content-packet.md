## 2. Polished LinkedIn post

AI legal readiness often starts with a surprisingly ordinary object: a use register.

Not a 40-page policy. A working list that shows:

• where AI is being used
• who owns each use case
• what data flows into the tool
• whether outputs affect customers, employees, investors, or regulated decisions
• what vendor terms govern confidentiality, IP, security, indemnity, and audit rights
• what human review, recordkeeping, and escalation path exists

That register becomes the control layer for several legal conversations.

Product leaders can see which AI features are ready for launch and which need a legal/security pass first.

CFOs and GCs can surface contract and renewal risk before the vendor stack gets hard to unwind.

HR and risk leaders can separate casual productivity use from employment, consumer, or automated-decision use cases.

Founders can make diligence easier. Buyers and investors increasingly ask not just whether a company “uses AI,” but who controls it, what evidence exists, and where the risk is documented.

The move is not to over-lawyer every experiment. It is to make AI use visible enough that leadership can govern it.

## 3. Short email / newsletter draft

**Subject:** The AI use register is the legal operating layer

**Preview text:** A practical way to move from scattered AI adoption to controlled AI governance.

**Body:**

Many technology companies now have AI use in three places at once: employee productivity tools, vendor products, and AI-enabled customer-facing features.

The legal problem is often not that every use is high risk. The problem is that leadership cannot easily see which uses involve sensitive data, consequential decisions, product claims, contract commitments, IP ownership issues, or human-review obligations.

A simple AI use register changes the conversation. It gives the GC, CFO, product lead, HR/risk owner, and founder a shared map:

- What AI tools and features are being used?
- Who owns each use case?
- What data goes in and what output comes out?
- What vendor terms control confidentiality, IP, security, indemnity, and audit rights?
- What human review and evidence trail exists?

That is the beginning of a legal operating system, not a one-time memo.

## 9. Compliance and ethics check

**Soften before publication:**

- Avoid saying a company “must” keep an AI use register unless tied to a specific, verified legal requirement. Say “a practical readiness step,” “a useful control layer,” or “often a strong starting point.”
- Avoid implying Colorado law is settled or currently enforceable for every use case. Say Colorado’s AI/transparency framework is evolving and source status should be checked before publication.
- Avoid legal conclusions about whether any AI use is compliant, high-risk, patentable, protectable, or eligible.
- Avoid suggesting form responses create an attorney-client relationship or constitute legal advice.
- Do not ask for confidential facts in public forms; request non-confidential, high-level categories only.
- Make clear that NIST AI RMF is a governance framework, not itself a legal mandate.
