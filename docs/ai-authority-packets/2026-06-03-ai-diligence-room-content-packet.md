## 2. LinkedIn post draft

AI due diligence is becoming less about whether a company “uses AI” and more about whether leadership can show how AI is being used.

For a Colorado or Mountain West tech company, the practical diligence question is often simple:

Can you produce the AI file?

Not a 40-page policy. A working evidence set:

• AI-enabled product features and internal tools
• owners for material use cases
• data inputs and customer/employee exposure
• vendor terms for confidentiality, IP ownership, security, audit, and indemnity
• human review paths for consequential outputs
• invention records showing where people — not tools — contributed to protectable work
• customer-facing claims and marketing guardrails
• board/investor-ready notes on what is known, unknown, and being remediated

That file helps in three moments: enterprise sales, financing, and acquisition diligence. It also reduces the odds that AI risk becomes a late-stage scramble handled by product, legal, HR, finance, and security in separate conversations.

The point is not to stop responsible AI use. It is to make AI use visible enough that leadership can govern it, contract around it, and explain it when a buyer, investor, customer, regulator, or insurer asks.

If your company could not assemble this file in a week, the gap may be operational before it is legal.

## 3. Short email / newsletter draft

**Subject:** Could your company produce the AI diligence file?

**Preview text:** A practical AI evidence file can reduce friction before enterprise sales, financing, or acquisition diligence.

**Body:**

AI diligence is shifting from abstract policy questions to practical evidence questions.

A buyer, investor, enterprise customer, or insurer may not ask whether your company has a perfect AI policy. They may ask what AI-enabled features exist, which internal tools are in use, what data flows into them, what vendor terms control confidentiality and IP ownership, and whether consequential outputs receive meaningful human review.

For technology companies, the useful move is to assemble a non-confidential AI diligence file before the request list arrives. The file does not need to expose trade secrets or customer information. It should make the operating layer visible: use cases, owners, data categories, vendor contracts, invention records, product claims, and remediation priorities.

That helps leadership treat AI readiness as a business operating system — not a last-minute legal scramble.
