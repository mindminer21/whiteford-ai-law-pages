## 1. Content angle and executive hook

**Angle:** AI-assisted invention records should be treated as executive IP infrastructure, not lab paperwork.

**Executive hook:** Before an AI-enabled feature becomes a customer demo, investor story, or diligence exhibit, leadership should be able to show the human contribution file: the problem framed by people, the experiments and tradeoffs they directed, the model’s role, what was accepted or rejected, and who owns the resulting IP stack.

## 2. Polished LinkedIn post

A practical AI patent issue for Colorado and Mountain West technology companies is not “Did we use AI?” It is “Can we show what the people contributed?”

The USPTO’s current posture is useful for executives: AI-assisted inventions are not automatically outside the patent system, but inventorship still turns on natural-person contribution. In plain business terms, the record matters.

Before an AI-enabled feature becomes a customer demo, investor story, or diligence exhibit, leadership should be able to point to a short invention file:

• technical problem the team was solving
• humans who framed the architecture, experiments, constraints, and tradeoffs
• what the AI system generated or accelerated
• outputs rejected, modified, validated, or implemented
• ownership of code, data, model outputs, contractor work, and roadmap

That file is not busywork. It helps patent counsel evaluate inventorship, eligibility, claim direction, trade-secret boundaries, and ownership before the company must reconstruct the story under pressure.

The opportunity: use AI aggressively, but document the human judgment that turns tool output into company IP.

Draft-only, not legal advice. AI patent strategy is fact-specific and should be evaluated before public disclosure or filing deadlines.

## 3. Short email / newsletter draft

**Subject:** The AI patent record to build before the demo  
**Preview text:** If AI helped create the product, the executive question is whether the human contribution can be shown clearly.

**Body:**

Founders and product leaders do not need to treat every AI-assisted experiment like a legal memo. But when an AI-enabled feature may become part of the company’s IP story, a lightweight record can prevent avoidable friction later.

The practical question is not simply whether AI was used. It is whether the company can explain the human contribution: who framed the technical problem, directed the experiments, made architecture decisions, selected or rejected outputs, validated the implementation, and connected the work to the product roadmap.

That record can support a better conversation about inventorship, claim strategy, patent eligibility, trade-secret boundaries, contractor assignments, vendor terms, and diligence readiness. It also helps avoid a common scaling problem: trying to reconstruct important R&D decisions months later, after a demo, disclosure, financing, customer negotiation, or acquisition conversation has already started.

This is not about slowing technical teams down. It is about giving leadership a repeatable way to preserve the business value of AI-assisted work.
