## Packet 1 — AI-assisted invention records / USPTO human contribution

### Title

AI Helped Build It. What Human Contribution Should Your Patent Record Show?

Supporting SEO phrases:

- AI-assisted invention
- AI patent lawyer
- USPTO AI inventorship guidance
- human contribution patent inventorship
- can AI be an inventor
- AI patent strategy scorecard

Thumbnail hook:

AI helped. Can the human story be shown?

### Audience and promise

Audience: technical founders, CTOs, R&D leaders, product leaders, inventors, and executives using AI tools during product development.

Educational promise: help viewers organize a non-confidential human-contribution and invention-record checklist before a patent-strategy conversation, without giving an inventorship, patentability, ownership, eligibility, filing-deadline, or trade-secret opinion.

### 30-second cold open

If AI helped your team build the invention, the patent conversation usually should not start with a yes-or-no question like, “Was AI involved?”

A more useful starting point is: what did the humans contribute, and can that contribution be explained from the record?

Who framed the problem? Who selected the architecture? Who evaluated alternatives? Who tested the result? Who made the technical choices that turned an AI-assisted output into a working solution?

In this video, I’ll walk through the non-confidential records founders should organize before a patent review — not to decide inventorship on YouTube, but to make the first strategy conversation more focused, safer, and more useful.

### 6-part talking outline

1. AI use is not the whole patent question
   - State that AI use alone is not the strategy answer.
   - Frame the practical inquiry around human technical contribution and documentation.
   - Use safe examples: coding assistants, model outputs, design tools, simulation tools, prior-art tools, analytics, and internal copilots.
   - Caveat: do not invite viewers to post invention facts in comments.

2. Separate tool activity from human contribution
   - Tool activity: prompt, output, code suggestion, simulated design, generated drawing, model recommendation.
   - Human contribution categories: problem framing, constraint selection, architecture, design alternatives, experimental plan, validation, error correction, implementation choice, tradeoff selection.
   - Say the record should help counsel ask better questions, not let the founder self-adjudicate inventorship.

3. Build a dated invention timeline
   - Suggested non-confidential internal timeline fields:
     - problem identified
     - initial concept
     - AI tool category used
     - human decision point
     - prototype or test milestone
     - technical improvement identified
     - public disclosure / demo / pitch / paper / GitHub / sales-deck checkpoint
   - Distinguish internal records from public disclosure.

4. Flag ownership and confidentiality issues early
   - Employee, contractor, consultant, university, accelerator, customer-funded, open-source, vendor-platform, and prior-employer flags.
   - Ask viewers to flag categories, not upload contracts or source code.
   - Explain that patent, trade-secret, assignment, and contract questions often need to be reviewed together.

5. Organize the first counsel conversation
   - Non-confidential pre-call checklist:
     - contributor roster by role
     - development timeline
     - AI tool categories used
     - human decision points
     - prototype/test milestones
     - disclosure dates/categories
     - ownership/assignment flags
     - current business goal: patent, trade secret, fundraising, licensing, product launch, sale, or partnership
   - Explain that a protected channel and conflict checks come before confidential details.

6. CTA: run the AI Patent Strategy Scorecard
   - Position the scorecard as a narrow diagnostic for documentation readiness.
   - Invite categorical, non-confidential answers only.
   - Connect the scorecard to the AI Patent Strategy Sprint if appropriate after attorney review and opt-in.

## Packet 2 — Colorado AI Act June 30, 2026 readiness / ADMT inventory

### Title

Colorado AI Act Readiness: What to Inventory Before June 30, 2026

Supporting SEO phrases:

- Colorado AI Act readiness
- Colorado AI compliance lawyer
- ADMT inventory
- high-risk AI systems
- AI compliance gameplan
- AI governance for Colorado companies

Thumbnail hook:

June 30, 2026: inventory first.

### Audience and promise

Audience: Colorado and Mountain West founders, CEOs, COOs, CFOs, GCs, HR leaders, product leaders, risk leaders, and AI program owners using, buying, or embedding automated decision systems.

Educational promise: help viewers build a non-confidential first-pass ADMT / AI use-case inventory so attorney review can focus on likely risk areas, without giving compliance status, statutory-coverage, discrimination-risk, or operational adequacy conclusions.

### 30-second cold open

Colorado’s AI law timeline has moved, but that does not mean companies should wait until the last minute.

The practical first step is usually not a policy. It is an inventory.

Where is AI being used? Is it touching hiring, lending, housing, healthcare, education, insurance, pricing, customer eligibility, fraud review, product decisions, or internal workforce decisions? Which systems are built in-house, which are vendor tools, and where is human review actually happening?

In this video, I’ll walk through a non-confidential inventory structure Colorado companies can start now before a legal readiness review — without trying to decide compliance status in a YouTube video.

### 6-part talking outline

1. The timeline moved, but readiness should not freeze
   - Explain that the selected topic is about readiness for June 30, 2026.
   - Avoid overclaiming statutory text; tell attorney reviewer to source-current final timing and obligations before publication.
   - Position the episode as operational issue spotting: inventory, owners, vendors, data, human review, records.

2. Start with use cases, not policy language
   - Inventory every AI or algorithmic system by business workflow.
   - Categories to include:
     - hiring / HR
     - credit / lending / payments
     - housing / tenant screening
     - education / training
     - healthcare / benefits
     - insurance
     - pricing / eligibility / fraud / account action
     - customer support or sales routing
     - product features using user data
   - Caveat: not every use case will be legally equivalent; attorney review decides legal classification.

3. Identify developer, deployer, and vendor posture
   - Is the company building the system, deploying a third-party system, customizing a vendor tool, reselling an AI feature, or embedding AI into a customer-facing product?
   - Record vendor name, contract owner, data owner, security/privacy owner, business owner, and escalation owner.
   - Flag contractual topics: data rights, confidentiality, audit, indemnity, training-use restrictions, output ownership, security, incident notice.

4. Map decision impact and human review
   - Use non-confidential fields:
     - decision or recommendation type
     - affected audience
     - consequence if wrong
     - human review point
     - override authority
     - notice / appeal / escalation pathway
     - records retained
   - Avoid saying a workflow is or is not “high risk” without attorney review.

5. Build a 30/60/90-day readiness board
   - 30 days: inventory, owners, vendor list, obvious no-confidential-data rules.
   - 60 days: contract review, data flow map, human review / escalation map, documentation gap list.
   - 90 days: governance cadence, training, board/leadership reporting, source-current legal review, prioritized remediation.
   - Position this as a readiness workstream, not a guarantee of compliance.

6. CTA: AI Compliance Gameplan Builder
   - Invite viewers to use the gameplan for non-confidential use-case categories and readiness scoring.
   - Connect to AI Compliance Readiness Sprint after opt-in and attorney review.
   - Reinforce no confidential facts in public forms or comments.

Educational promise: help viewers organize a non-confidential AI/data/IP diligence file before buyer, investor, or partner requests arrive, without giving deal-success, valuation, ownership, compliance, privacy, antitrust, or risk-allocation conclusions.

### 30-second cold open

In tech deals, the diligence issue that slows the process is not always financial.

Sometimes it is: Who owns the code? Who assigned the invention? What data trained the model? What customer promises were made? Which vendor terms limit transfer, audit, or use? Can the company explain how AI is used in the product?

If you wait until a buyer asks, you may be reconstructing the story under deadline pressure.

In this video, I’ll walk through a non-confidential AI, data, and IP diligence file founders can organize before a transaction process — not to predict a deal outcome, but to reduce avoidable friction.

### 6-part talking outline

1. Deals slow down when the technical/legal story is scattered
   - Explain that buyer diligence often tests whether company claims can be backed by records.
   - Use categories only: IP ownership, data rights, customer/vendor terms, AI product claims, privacy/security, employment/contractor records.
   - Avoid stating what will or will not affect valuation or closing.

2. Build the IP ownership file
   - Organize:
     - patent/application list and invention timeline categories
     - employee and contractor assignment status
     - founder assignment documents
     - open-source inventory / policy
     - customer-funded development flags
     - university, government, accelerator, or joint-development flags
     - trade-secret access controls
   - Caveat: do not ask viewers to send documents through public forms.

3. Build the AI and data-use file
   - Organize:
     - AI features in the product
     - AI tools used internally
     - training, fine-tuning, embedding, analytics, or retrieval workflows
     - data sources by category
     - customer data, personal data, sensitive data, or third-party data flags
     - model/vendor terms and restrictions
     - logs, prompts, outputs, evaluation, and human review categories
   - Do not ask for datasets, prompts, code, credentials, or model weights publicly.

4. Review contracts for diligence friction
   - Categories to identify:
     - customer assignment/change-of-control restrictions
     - data-processing obligations
     - confidentiality and security commitments
     - audit rights
     - AI-use representations
     - vendor termination / transfer limits
     - indemnity, warranty, limitation-of-liability provisions
   - Explain that deal counsel must review actual agreements through the right channel.

5. Make the cleanup work visible before outreach
   - Create a founder readiness board:
     - green: complete and easy to explain
     - yellow: needs counsel review
     - red: missing, inconsistent, or deadline-sensitive
   - Prioritize before banker/buyer/investor pressure.
   - Map issues to Whiteford resources: IP, corporate/M&A, privacy/data, employment, commercial contracts, litigation if needed.

6. CTA: Founder M&A Readiness Checklist
   - Position the checklist as a narrow diagnostic for issue spotting before a transaction or strategic partnership.
   - Also route companies needing broader operating cleanup to the Colorado Tech Company Legal Health Checklist.
   - Reinforce no confidential upload through public channels.
