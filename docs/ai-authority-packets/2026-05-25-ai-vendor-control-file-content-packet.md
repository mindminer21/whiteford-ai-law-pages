## 1. Content angle and executive hook

**Angle:** AI vendor risk should be managed through an executive “AI Vendor Control File,” not scattered contract emails, product notes, and policy fragments.

**Executive hook:** If an enterprise customer, board member, buyer, CFO, or regulator asked tomorrow which AI vendors touch company data or customer-facing workflows, leadership should be able to show one practical file: owner, use case, data flow, contract rights, human review, escalation path, and evidence.

## 2. LinkedIn post draft

AI vendor risk is usually not solved by a policy first. It is solved by a control file.

For Colorado and Mountain West technology companies, the practical question is concrete: if an AI vendor touches customer data, employee data, product decisions, source code, model outputs, or regulated workflows, who owns the review?

A useful AI Vendor Control File should show:

• the business owner and use case
• what data the tool receives and returns
• whether the vendor can train on company or customer material
• confidentiality, security, audit, indemnity, and retention terms
• where human review is required before decisions or customer-facing outputs
• how the tool fits into product, HR, sales, or finance workflows
• what evidence leadership can produce for an enterprise customer, board, buyer, or regulator

This is not about slowing product teams down. It is about giving them a repeatable path to say yes safely.

Colorado’s AI law timeline remains a reason to build readiness, not to wait. NIST’s AI RMF gives useful nonbinding governance vocabulary. But the executive operating layer is usually local: contracts, ownership, data flow, approval rights, and a documented escalation path.

If your company is using AI faster than your legal operating system can see it, start with the vendor file.

## 3. Short email / newsletter draft

**Subject:** The AI vendor file your leadership team should be able to produce  
**Preview text:** Before the policy, build the control file: vendors, data, terms, owners, human review, and evidence.

**Body:**

AI vendor risk often starts quietly: a product team tests a model provider, sales adopts an AI note-taker, HR evaluates a screening workflow, finance uses an analytics tool, or engineering connects AI to source code.

The legal issue is not simply whether the company “uses AI.” The more useful executive question is whether the company can see and govern the vendor stack.

A practical AI Vendor Control File should answer six questions:

1. Who owns the use case internally?
2. What data goes into and comes out of the tool?
3. Can the vendor train on company, customer, employee, or product material?
4. What do the confidentiality, security, audit, retention, indemnity, and IP terms actually say?
5. Where is human review required before the output affects customers, employees, regulated decisions, or public claims?
6. What evidence could leadership produce during enterprise procurement, financing, M&A diligence, or a compliance review?

This is the kind of bounded legal operating work that helps technology companies move faster with fewer surprises.
