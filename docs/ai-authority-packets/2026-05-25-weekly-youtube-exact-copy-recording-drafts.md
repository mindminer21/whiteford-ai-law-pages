## Tight 6-part outline / teleprompter bullet pass

### 30-second cold open

If AI helped build your product, the patent conversation should not start and end with one question: “Did you use AI?”

A better first question is: what did the humans contribute, and can the record explain it?

Who framed the problem? Who selected the architecture? Who decided which model output was wrong? Who tested the result? Who made the technical choices that turned assistance into an invention story?

In this video, I’ll walk through the non-confidential file a founder can organize before a patent-strategy review — not to decide inventorship on YouTube, but to make the first legal conversation safer, faster, and more useful.

### 1. AI involvement is not the whole patent answer

Current USPTO materials, including the November 2025 revised inventorship guidance, say the same inventorship standard applies to all inventions regardless of whether AI systems were used, and that there is no separate or modified standard for AI-assisted inventions.

AI systems are treated as tools used by human inventors; only natural persons can be named as inventors.

For founders, the practical point is not panic and not self-certification. The practical point is documentation.

Do not post invention details, source code, prompts, model outputs, drawings, claim ideas, customer data, contracts, or disclosure dates in comments or public forms.

### 2. Separate tool activity from human technical contribution

Tool activity might include prompts, generated alternatives, suggested code, model recommendations, search outputs, simulations, summaries, analytics, or internal copilot activity.

Human contribution categories might include problem framing, constraints, architecture, feature selection, experiment design, technical tradeoffs, validation, error correction, implementation decisions, and performance improvements.

The point is not to decide legal significance publicly. The point is to help counsel see the right facts through a protected attorney-review channel.

### 3. Build the AI-assisted invention chronology

A useful non-confidential chronology can organize categories like:

- problem or customer need category
- initial concept date category
- AI tool category used
- human decision point
- prototype or test milestone
- technical improvement category
- public disclosure, demo, pitch, paper, GitHub, or sales-deck checkpoint
- contributor roles

Dates and roles help counsel ask better questions. They do not let a founder self-certify inventorship, patentability, eligibility, ownership, or filing strategy.

### 4. Add ownership, assignment, and confidentiality flags

The patent file should also flag ownership and assignment categories:

- founder, employee, contractor, consultant, university, accelerator, customer-funded, government-funded, open-source, vendor-platform, prior-employer, and joint-development issues

Those flags can connect patent strategy with trade secret treatment, contractor assignments, vendor terms, product roadmap, disclosure timing, and financing or diligence planning.

Do not upload agreements or confidential documents through a public scorecard or YouTube comment.

### 5. Decide what belongs in the first attorney-review conversation

Before a protected legal review, a founder can organize non-confidential categories:

- contributor roster by role
- development timeline
- AI tool categories
- human technical decision points
- prototype and testing milestones
- ownership and assignment flags
- public-disclosure categories
- business objective: patent filing, trade secret, fundraising, licensing, enterprise customer, sale, or strategic partnership

Confidential facts should wait until conflicts and engagement procedures are complete.

## Tight 6-part outline / teleprompter bullet pass

### 30-second cold open

If your Colorado company is trying to prepare for AI regulation, the first document probably should not be a generic policy copied from the internet.

The first document should be an inventory.

Where is AI being used? What business decision does it support? Is it internal or customer-facing? Is the system built in-house or bought from a vendor? What data goes in? Who owns the contract? Where does human review actually happen?

In this video, I’ll walk through a non-confidential inventory structure for Colorado AI readiness — not to decide whether your company is compliant, but to give leadership and counsel a real starting point.

### 1. The law may move, but readiness should not freeze

Current official Colorado sources show SB24-205 became law, SB25B-004 extended the effective date of SB24-205 requirements to June 30, 2026, and the Colorado Attorney General’s ADAI page says the law’s provisions go into effect June 30, 2026.

The AG page also says formal rulemaking has not begun and that future rulemaking input opportunities will be posted after notice.

That is why this video focuses on readiness categories — inventory, owners, vendors, data, human review, records, and governance cadence — not compliance conclusions.

### 2. Start with use cases, not policy language

Inventory AI systems by workflow category:

- hiring and HR
- lending, credit, or payments
- housing or tenant screening
- healthcare or benefits
- insurance
- education or training
- fraud or account action
- pricing or eligibility
- customer support or sales routing
- product features using user data
- internal productivity copilots

Not every use case is legally equivalent. Counsel determines classification, coverage, and required controls after reviewing actual facts through the right channel.

### 3. Identify developer, deployer, and vendor posture

Ask whether the company is building the system, deploying a third-party tool, customizing a vendor tool, embedding an AI feature, reselling AI, or using AI internally.

Name owner categories:

- business owner
- technical owner
- data/privacy owner
- contract owner
- security owner
- escalation owner

Flag contracts for data rights, confidentiality, training-use restrictions, audit, indemnity, security, incident notice, output ownership, and termination or portability.

### 4. Map decision impact and human review

Use non-confidential fields:

- decision or recommendation category
- affected audience
- consequence if wrong
- human review point
- override authority
- notice or escalation pathway
- records retained
- vendor documentation available

Avoid saying a system is or is not high risk, covered, compliant, discriminatory, or legally sufficient in a public video.

### 5. Turn the inventory into a 30/60/90-day readiness board

30 days: inventory, owners, vendor list, no-confidential-data rules, and intake form.

60 days: vendor contract review, data-flow map, human-review map, and records gap list.

90 days: governance cadence, training, leadership reporting, source-current legal review, and remediation priorities.

NIST AI RMF can provide voluntary governance vocabulary — govern, map, measure, manage — but it is not a legal safe harbor or proof of compliance.

## Tight 6-part outline / teleprompter bullet pass

### 30-second cold open

The AI risk that shows up in diligence is not always the model.

Sometimes it is the contract.

What did the vendor promise? What did you promise your customers? Can the vendor use your data for training? Can you transfer the agreement in a deal? Who owns outputs or improvements? Did your privacy notice say something different from your product roadmap?

In this video, I’ll walk through the non-confidential AI vendor and data terms file a tech company can organize before an enterprise sale, financing, partnership, or acquisition process.

### 1. AI adoption creates a contract stack, not just a tool stack

Every AI tool may sit inside vendor terms, privacy terms, data-processing terms, security terms, customer promises, internal policies, and product claims.

The issue is not simply whether the tool is useful. It is whether the company can explain rights, restrictions, controls, and promises.

Keep examples categorical. Do not interpret any viewer’s contract in a public video.

### 2. Map vendor data rights before the next renewal

Categories to identify:

- input data categories
- output ownership or use language
- training, fine-tuning, and product-improvement rights
- data retention and deletion
- confidentiality
- security controls
- audit or reporting rights
- subcontractors and third-party sharing
- incident notice

FTC public guidance warns that retroactive or hidden changes to terms or privacy policies that expand data sharing or AI training practices can create unfair/deceptive-practice risk. Use that as issue-spotting context, not as a conclusion about any vendor or contract.

### 3. Compare vendor terms against customer and privacy promises

Ask whether customer contracts, DPAs, privacy notices, security questionnaires, sales decks, product documentation, or AI feature claims make promises about data use, training, sharing, confidentiality, security, or AI functionality.

FTC Operation AI Comply reinforces that AI claims do not receive an exemption from ordinary consumer-protection rules.

For a technology company, the practical takeaway is consistency review: map the promises before renewal, enterprise sales, financing, or diligence.

### 4. Turn the AI contract stack into an outside-GC triage board

Green: known tool, current owner, terms saved, data categories known, customer promises checked.

Yellow: attorney review needed, renewal coming, unclear training/use term, unclear output ownership, possible customer-promise mismatch.

Red: no contract owner, missing DPA/security terms, sensitive data involved, enterprise customer asks pending, acquisition or investor diligence coming.

This is operating-system cleanup, not legal advice or contract interpretation.

### 5. Make the diligence file before a buyer asks

Organize non-confidential diligence categories:

- AI vendor list
- business owner and technical owner
- data categories
- customer commitments
- privacy notice checkpoints
- security documentation
- model or product claims
- assignment and change-of-control restrictions
- open-source or third-party dependency flags

If DOJ/FTC Merger Guidelines are mentioned, use them only as broad, non-binding transaction-review context. Do not predict any viewer’s transaction outcome.
