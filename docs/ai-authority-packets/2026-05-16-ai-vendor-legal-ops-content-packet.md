## 2. LinkedIn post draft (900–1400 characters)

AI adoption is rarely a single legal issue. It usually starts as a quiet operating-system problem.

Sales adds a meeting summarizer. Product tests a model in a feature. HR reviews a screening tool. Finance tries a forecasting platform. Each use may be reasonable on its own. The legal risk comes from not knowing who owns the intake, what data is touched, which vendor terms apply, and whether the use could affect a consequential decision.

For Colorado and Mountain West technology companies, the first move is not a 40-page AI policy. It is a practical AI vendor and use-case register tied to contracts, data, product launch, employment, and board reporting.

That register should answer:
• Which AI tools are in active use or pilot?
• What data, IP, customer commitments, or employment decisions do they touch?
• Which contracts control confidentiality, training rights, indemnity, audit, and output ownership?
• Who can approve a higher-risk use before launch?
• What evidence would we show a customer, investor, or regulator if asked?

Colorado’s AI landscape is still moving, but waiting for perfect certainty is usually not a strategy. The companies easiest to finance, sell to enterprise customers, or diligence are the ones that can explain their system calmly.

Draft-only thought: treat AI governance as part of your legal operating system, not as a one-time policy project.

- Do not state definitive Colorado AI Act obligations without a fresh status check and attorney review; use "Colorado’s AI landscape is moving" and cite official sources.
