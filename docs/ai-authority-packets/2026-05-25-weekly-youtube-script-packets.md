## Packet 1 — AI-assisted invention: no special inventorship shortcut

### Title

AI Helped Build the Product. What Should Your Patent Strategy File Show?

Supporting SEO phrases:

- AI-assisted invention
- AI patent strategy
- AI patent lawyer
- USPTO AI inventorship FAQ

- AI invention documentation

Thumbnail hook:

AI can assist. The human story still matters.

### Audience / dream customer

Technical founders, CTOs, R&D leaders, product leaders, inventors, and executives at Colorado or Mountain West AI-enabled product companies who are using generative AI, coding assistants, model outputs, simulations, analytics, or other AI tools during product development.

### Core pain / desired outcome

Pain: the team used AI in R&D and now worries that the patent conversation will become a confusing yes/no fight over whether AI was involved.

Outcome: the viewer leaves with a non-confidential issue map for organizing the human-contribution record, ownership flags, and patent/trade-secret strategy questions before a protected attorney review.

### Educational promise

Help viewers organize the categories a patent-strategy file should contain when AI tools were used in development, without giving an inventorship, patentability, eligibility, ownership, filing-deadline, disclosure, or trade-secret opinion.

### 30-second cold open

If AI helped build your product, the patent conversation should not start and end with one question: “Did you use AI?”

A better first question is: what did the humans contribute, and can the record explain it?

Who framed the problem? Who selected the architecture? Who decided which model output was wrong? Who tested the result? Who made the technical choices that turned assistance into an invention story?

In this video, I’ll walk through the non-confidential file a founder can organize before a patent-strategy review — not to decide inventorship on YouTube, but to make the first legal conversation safer, faster, and more useful.

### 6-part talking outline

1. AI involvement is not the whole patent answer
   - Public USPTO FAQ materials explain that the inventorship analysis focuses on human contribution and that AI-assisted inventions do not get a heightened or separate standard.
   - Translate that into a founder operating point: do not panic, but do not leave the story undocumented.
   - Use categorical examples only: coding assistants, image/design tools, AI search, simulations, analytics, model recommendations, and internal copilots.
   - Caveat: do not post invention details, source code, prompts, model outputs, drawings, claim ideas, or disclosure dates in comments.

2. Separate tool activity from human technical contribution
   - Tool activity: prompts, generated alternatives, suggested code, model recommendations, search outputs, simulations, summaries, analytics.
   - Human contribution categories: problem framing, constraints, architecture, feature selection, experiment design, technical tradeoffs, validation, error correction, implementation decisions, and performance improvements.
   - Make clear that counsel decides legal significance after reviewing facts through a protected channel.

3. Build the AI-assisted invention chronology
   - Non-confidential internal fields:
     - problem or customer need category
     - initial concept date category
     - AI tool category used
     - human decision point
     - prototype/test milestone
     - technical improvement category
     - public disclosure/demo/pitch/paper/GitHub/sales-deck checkpoint
     - contributor roles
   - Explain why dates and roles help counsel ask better questions.
   - Avoid suggesting that the viewer can self-certify inventorship or patentability.

4. Add ownership, assignment, and confidentiality flags
   - Employee, founder, contractor, consultant, university, accelerator, customer-funded, government-funded, open-source, vendor-platform, prior-employer, and joint-development flags.
   - Connect patent, trade secret, contractor assignment, vendor terms, and product roadmap.
   - Do not ask viewers to send contracts or upload documents through public forms.

5. Decide what belongs in the first attorney-review conversation
   - Suggested non-confidential pre-call categories:
     - contributor roster by role
     - development timeline
     - AI tool categories
     - human technical decision points
     - prototype and testing milestones
     - ownership/assignment flags
     - public-disclosure categories
     - business objective: patent filing, trade secret, fundraising, licensing, enterprise customer, sale, or strategic partnership
   - Explain that confidential facts wait until conflicts and engagement procedures are complete.

6. CTA: AI Patent Strategy Scorecard
   - Position the scorecard as a narrow diagnostic for documentation readiness, not a legal determination.
   - Invite non-confidential categorical answers only.
   - If the viewer opts in, the follow-up can route to an attorney-reviewed AI Patent Strategy Sprint discussion.

## Packet 2 — Colorado AI readiness: inventory before policy

### Title

Colorado AI Readiness: Build the Inventory Before You Write the Policy

Supporting SEO phrases:

- Colorado AI compliance lawyer

Inventory first. Policy second.

### Audience / dream customer

Colorado and Mountain West founders, CEOs, COOs, CFOs, GCs, HR leaders, product leaders, compliance leads, risk owners, and AI program owners whose companies are using, buying, or embedding AI in customer, employee, product, credit, housing, healthcare, insurance, education, or operational workflows.

### Core pain / desired outcome

Pain: leaders hear changing Colorado AI law dates and obligations and either overreact with a generic policy or freeze until the deadline is closer.

Outcome: the viewer leaves with a practical, non-confidential AI use-case inventory structure that can support counsel review, vendor review, records work, and 30/60/90-day readiness planning.

### Educational promise

Help viewers build a first-pass AI and automated-decision inventory so legal review can focus on the right systems, owners, vendors, data flows, human-review points, and records — without deciding statutory coverage, compliance status, discrimination risk, or operational sufficiency in a YouTube video.

### 30-second cold open

If your Colorado company is trying to prepare for AI regulation, the first document probably should not be a generic policy copied from the internet.

The first document should be an inventory.

Where is AI being used? What business decision does it support? Is it internal or customer-facing? Is the system built in-house or bought from a vendor? What data goes in? Who owns the contract? Where does human review actually happen?

In this video, I’ll walk through a non-confidential inventory structure for Colorado AI readiness — not to decide whether your company is compliant, but to give leadership and counsel a real starting point.

### 6-part talking outline

1. The law may move, but readiness should not freeze
   - Colorado’s official bill pages show SB24-205 became law and SB25B-004 extended key effective timing to no later than June 30, 2026.
   - Avoid overclaiming obligations; final copy must be source-current on publication day.
   - Frame the episode as readiness work: inventory, owners, vendors, data, human review, records, notices, and governance cadence.

2. Start with use cases, not policy language
   - Inventory AI systems by workflow:
     - hiring / HR
     - lending / credit / payments
     - housing / tenant screening
     - healthcare / benefits
     - insurance
     - education / training
     - fraud / account action
     - pricing / eligibility
     - customer support / sales routing
     - product features using user data
     - internal productivity copilots
   - Caveat: not every use case is legally equivalent; counsel determines classification.

3. Identify developer, deployer, and vendor posture
   - Is the company building the system, deploying a third-party tool, customizing a vendor tool, embedding an AI feature, reselling AI, or using AI internally?
   - Record owner categories:
     - business owner
     - technical owner
     - data/privacy owner
     - contract owner
     - security owner
     - escalation owner
   - Flag contracts for data rights, confidentiality, training-use restrictions, audit, indemnity, security, incident notice, output ownership, and termination/portability.

4. Map decision impact and human review
   - Non-confidential fields:
     - decision/recommendation category
     - affected audience
     - consequence if wrong
     - human review point
     - override authority
     - notice or escalation pathway
     - records retained
     - vendor documentation available
   - Avoid saying a system is or is not high risk or compliant.

5. Turn the inventory into a 30/60/90-day readiness board
   - 30 days: inventory, owners, vendor list, no-confidential-data rules, intake form.
   - 60 days: vendor contract review, data-flow map, human-review map, records gap list.
   - 90 days: governance cadence, training, leadership reporting, source-current legal review, remediation priorities.
   - Use NIST AI RMF language as a governance reference point: govern, map, measure, manage — but do not present it as a legal safe harbor.

6. CTA: AI Compliance Gameplan Builder
   - Invite viewers to answer non-confidential use-case and readiness questions.
   - Position the builder as a narrow diagnostic: where are the readiness gaps before the first legal meeting?
   - Any follow-up should be opt-in-only and attorney-review-gated.

## Packet 3 — AI vendor/data terms: the hidden operating and diligence risk

### Title

Your AI Vendor Terms May Become a Deal Diligence Problem

Supporting SEO phrases:

- AI vendor contract review

- Colorado tech general counsel

Thumbnail hook:

The AI contract may be the diligence issue.

### Audience / dream customer

Founder-led and venture-backed technology companies, SaaS companies, AI product teams, CEOs, CFOs, CTOs, COOs, legal operators, product leaders, and founders preparing for enterprise sales, fundraising, strategic partnerships, or acquisition discussions.

### Core pain / desired outcome

Pain: teams adopt AI tools quickly, but vendor terms, privacy promises, training-use restrictions, customer commitments, and data rights are scattered across contracts and policies until a buyer, investor, enterprise customer, or counsel asks for them.

Outcome: the viewer leaves with a non-confidential AI vendor/data terms issue map that supports outside-GC triage and pre-deal diligence cleanup.

### Educational promise

Help viewers identify the categories of AI vendor and data terms that can create operational, customer, privacy, and diligence friction — without giving a contract interpretation, privacy opinion, compliance opinion, valuation opinion, or deal-success prediction.

### 30-second cold open

The AI risk that shows up in diligence is not always the model.

Sometimes it is the contract.

What did the vendor promise? What did you promise your customers? Can the vendor use your data for training? Can you transfer the agreement in a deal? Who owns outputs or improvements? Did your privacy notice say something different from your product roadmap?

In this video, I’ll walk through the non-confidential AI vendor and data terms file a tech company can organize before an enterprise sale, financing, partnership, or acquisition process.

### 6-part talking outline

1. AI adoption creates a contract stack, not just a tool stack
   - Every AI tool may sit inside vendor terms, privacy terms, data-processing terms, security terms, customer promises, internal policies, and product claims.
   - The problem is not simply whether the tool is good; it is whether the company can explain rights, restrictions, and controls.
   - Keep all examples categorical and non-confidential.

2. Map vendor data rights before the next renewal
   - Categories to identify:
     - input data categories
     - output ownership/use language
     - training, fine-tuning, product-improvement rights
     - data retention and deletion
     - confidentiality
     - security controls
     - audit/reporting rights
     - subcontractors and third-party sharing
     - incident notice
   - FTC public guidance flags risk around quiet, retroactive changes to privacy or terms that expand AI training or data-sharing practices.
   - Do not interpret any viewer’s contract publicly.

3. Compare vendor terms against customer and privacy promises
   - Ask whether customer contracts, DPAs, privacy notices, security questionnaires, sales decks, or product documentation make promises about data use, training, sharing, confidentiality, or AI features.
   - FTC Operation AI Comply is a public reminder that AI claims remain subject to ordinary unfair/deceptive-practice scrutiny; avoid hype and unsupported claims.
   - Position this as a consistency review, not a legal conclusion.

4. Turn the AI contract stack into an outside-GC triage board
   - Green: known tool, current owner, terms saved, data categories known, customer promises checked.
   - Yellow: attorney review needed, renewal coming, unclear training/use term, unclear output ownership, customer promise mismatch possible.
   - Red: no contract owner, missing DPA/security terms, sensitive data involved, enterprise customer asks pending, acquisition/investor diligence coming.
   - Map to `/colorado-tech-general-counsel` and the Legal Health Checklist.

5. Make the diligence file before a buyer asks
   - Categories for a non-confidential diligence file:
     - AI vendor list
     - business owner and technical owner
     - data categories
     - customer commitments
     - privacy notice checkpoints
     - security documentation
     - model/product claims
     - assignment/change-of-control restrictions
     - open-source or third-party dependency flags
   - DOJ/FTC merger guidelines are only broad official transaction context; do not imply any specific deal raises antitrust issues.

6. CTA: Founder M&A Readiness Checklist or Tech Company Legal Health Checklist

   - For operating-system cleanup before a transaction: Colorado Tech Company Legal Health Checklist on `/colorado-tech-general-counsel#lead-magnet`.
