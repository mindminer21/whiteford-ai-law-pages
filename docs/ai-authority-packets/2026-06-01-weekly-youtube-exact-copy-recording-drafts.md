## Final title

AI Helped Build the Invention. What Human Contribution Should You Document?

## Thumbnail hook

AI helped. Humans still matter.

## Cold open

If AI helped build your invention, the first legal question should not be a panic question about whether you used AI.

The better first question is what the humans contributed and whether the record can explain that contribution in a protected attorney-review setting.

Who framed the technical problem?

Who selected the architecture?

Who rejected weak model outputs?

Who tested the prototype?

Who made the technical choices that turned AI assistance into a product direction?

In this video, I’ll walk through the non-confidential categories founders can organize before a patent-strategy review.

This is not a YouTube inventorship opinion, patentability opinion, eligibility opinion, ownership opinion, or filing-strategy opinion.

## Six-part teleprompter-friendly outline

### 1. Start with the current USPTO posture, not AI panic

Current USPTO materials say the same inventorship standard applies regardless of whether AI systems were used.

USPTO also says there is no separate or modified standard for AI-assisted inventions.

Only natural persons can be inventors.

AI systems are treated as tools used by human inventors.

For a founder, the practical takeaway is not to decide inventorship alone.

The practical takeaway is to organize the human-contribution record before counsel reviews it.

### 2. Separate AI tool activity from human technical choices

AI tool activity might include prompts, generated alternatives, summaries, code suggestions, simulations, analytics, model outputs, or design options.

Human technical choices might include problem framing, constraints, architecture, feature selection, experiment design, validation, error correction, integration, performance tuning, and implementation decisions.

The record should make it easier for counsel to see who did what.

The record should not try to turn a public checklist into a legal conclusion.

### 3. Build a non-confidential invention chronology

A useful chronology starts with categories, not secrets.

List the problem category, development milestone category, AI tool category, human decision point, prototype checkpoint, test result category, disclosure checkpoint, and contributor role.

Dates can help counsel ask better questions.

Roles can help counsel ask better questions.

Do not put source code, prompts, claim ideas, drawings, model outputs, customer data, unreleased features, contracts, or disclosure dates into comments or public forms.

### 4. Add ownership and confidentiality flags

The AI-assisted invention file should also flag ownership categories.

Those categories might include founder work, employee work, contractor work, consultant work, university work, accelerator work, customer-funded work, government-funded work, open-source inputs, vendor-platform terms, prior-employer issues, and joint-development issues.

Those flags connect patent strategy with assignments, trade secrets, vendor terms, product roadmap, financing, and diligence.

They do not decide the legal answer by themselves.

### 5. Prepare the first attorney-review conversation

Before a protected review, organize the contributor roster, development timeline, AI tool categories, human technical decision points, prototype milestones, testing milestones, ownership flags, public-disclosure categories, and business objective.

The business objective might be patent filing, trade-secret treatment, fundraising, licensing, enterprise sales, strategic partnership, or acquisition readiness.

Confidential details should wait until conflicts and engagement procedures are complete.

The goal is a safer first conversation, not a public diagnosis.

## Final title

AI, IP, and Data Questions Buyers Ask Before a Tech Acquisition

## Thumbnail hook

Clean up before diligence asks.

## Cold open

A buyer’s first hard questions may not be about AI strategy in the abstract.

They may be about who owns the code, who owns the inventions, what data trained the system, what the vendor terms allow, what customers were promised, and whether the company can prove the story in diligence.

If a founder waits until the request list arrives, the cleanup work can become more expensive and more stressful.

In this video, I’ll walk through a founder-facing AI, IP, and data diligence readiness checklist.

This is not a deal-success prediction, valuation opinion, securities advice, tax advice, antitrust advice, diligence pass/fail opinion, or legal advice.

## Six-part teleprompter-friendly outline

### 1. Reframe M&A readiness as evidence readiness

M&A readiness is not just whether the company has a strong story.

It is whether the company has organized evidence for the story.

For AI companies and AI-enabled products, that evidence often touches IP ownership, invention records, assignments, open-source use, vendor contracts, customer promises, privacy, security, data rights, and model governance.

The readiness goal is not to predict the deal.

The readiness goal is to reduce avoidable diligence friction.

### 2. Map IP ownership before the buyer asks

Start with invention assignment categories, contractor assignment categories, employee IP categories, founder contribution categories, patent filing categories, trade-secret controls, open-source records, and joint-development flags.

Do not wait until a buyer asks who created a core feature.

Do not assume that repository access proves ownership.

Do not upload assignments or confidential records through a public checklist.

Use non-confidential categories to prepare the attorney-review conversation.

### 3. Map AI and data provenance

Buyers may ask what data was used, where it came from, what rights the company has, and what restrictions apply.

They may ask whether customer data, third-party data, synthetic data, public data, licensed data, employee data, or vendor data touches the product.

They may ask whether outputs are used in customer-facing decisions, regulated workflows, or consequential decisions.

Those questions need careful review.

The founder readiness step is to identify the categories before the request list arrives.

### 4. Map vendor and customer promise friction

AI products often depend on vendors, platforms, APIs, models, cloud services, annotation tools, security tools, and data processors.

Diligence may focus on training rights, output ownership, confidentiality, security, audit rights, indemnity, usage restrictions, uptime promises, subcontractors, and change notices.

Customer promises matter too.

Marketing claims, service descriptions, privacy statements, DPAs, security exhibits, and enterprise contracts should be consistent with how the product actually works.

FTC guidance remains useful risk context for avoiding unsupported AI claims and quiet data-rights changes.

### 5. Build a non-confidential readiness checklist

A practical readiness checklist can track ownership records, invention records, AI-use register, data-source categories, model/vendor inventory, customer promise map, security and privacy records, open-source review status, key contract locations, and unresolved issues for counsel.

The checklist should identify what exists, what is missing, what is stale, and who owns the cleanup.

It should not ask founders to disclose confidential deal facts through a public form.

It should not promise a buyer response, valuation result, or closing outcome.
