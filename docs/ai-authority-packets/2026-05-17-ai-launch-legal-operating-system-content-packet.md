## 2. LinkedIn post draft — 1,390 characters

Colorado technology companies are starting to treat AI launch risk as a legal and commercial operating issue, not a policy memo.

That is the right move.

Product may be using an AI vendor. Engineering may have model-output logs. Sales may be promising automation. HR may be piloting decision-support tools. IP counsel may be assessing whether AI-assisted R&D created protectable inventions.

None of those questions lives in one lane.

Before an AI feature goes into a demo, board update, enterprise contract, or diligence folder, executives should answer four questions:

1. What AI systems are in the product or operating stack?
2. What data, rights, confidentiality, and vendor terms govern them?
3. What human review and escalation path exists for higher-risk decisions?
4. What IP and roadmap evidence would we want if a buyer, investor, regulator, or competitor asked later?

Colorado’s AI framework is moving, and federal AI patent guidance continues to emphasize human contribution. The point is not to overreact. The point is to build a legal operating layer that lets the business ship with cleaner records and fewer surprises.

For scaling AI companies, useful legal work may be a launch file: inventory, owner map, vendor/IP terms, decision-impact notes, and a 30/60/90-day cleanup plan.

Outside GC support should make that operational: a repeatable way to launch responsibly.

Founders and executives often ask for an “AI policy” when the more urgent need is operational: who owns the AI launch file?

- Which AI systems are in the product or internal stack?
- What vendor, confidentiality, data-use, ownership, and indemnity terms apply?

- What evidence would support IP, product, and diligence narratives later?

Colorado’s AI legal framework continues to evolve, and federal patent guidance continues to focus on human contribution for AI-assisted inventions. That uncertainty is a reason to build a cleaner operating layer, not a reason to freeze product work.
