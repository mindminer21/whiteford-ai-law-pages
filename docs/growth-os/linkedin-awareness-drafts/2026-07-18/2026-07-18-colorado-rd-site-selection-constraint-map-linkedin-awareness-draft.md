## 6. Draft LinkedIn caption for Jeff review

A tax incentive is not a location strategy.

On Thursday, Colorado's economic-development commission approved incentives for an anonymous quantum-computing company considering the Denver metro area for a roughly 80,000–100,000-square-foot R&D facility.

OEDIT says the project could involve more than $158 million in capital investment and 225 new jobs.

The company is also considering Chicago—and the state is explicit that an incentive offer does not guarantee a Colorado expansion.

That is the right reminder for any COO, CFO or founder comparing expansion sites:

**Score the operating constraints before you score the incentive.**

Put each finalist location across the top. Then test five questions:

1. **Talent**  
Which roles must be hired locally? How deep is the real candidate pool, and what happens if hiring takes twice as long as planned?

2. **Time to operate**  
Which permitting, utility, construction, equipment, housing or supply-chain dependency controls the date when useful work can actually begin?

3. **Ecosystem**  
Which universities, suppliers, customers, labs, partners and specialized services materially shorten the learning curve—not just improve the map of nearby logos?

4. **Eight-year economics**  
After wages, real estate, buildout, energy, travel, retention and the conditions attached to incentives, which location still wins under a conservative scenario?

5. **Reversibility**  
If demand, technology, funding or hiring changes, which commitments can be resized—and which become expensive anchors?

For every important assumption, assign one owner and one disconfirming test.

The best site is not necessarily the place with the largest offer.

It is the place where the company's hardest operating constraints become easiest to manage.

Educational only; not legal, tax, real-estate, employment, environmental, engineering, incentives or investment advice and not an attorney-client relationship.
