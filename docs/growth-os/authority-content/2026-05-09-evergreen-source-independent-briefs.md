## Brief 1 — AI Patent

Working title: “AI-Assisted Invention: The Founder’s Human-Contribution Check”

Authority pillar: AI Patent Law

Primary audience:

- Technical founders, CTOs, inventors, product leaders, and CEOs building AI-enabled products or using AI tools in R&D.
- Secondary: CFOs and GCs who need to understand whether invention records are ready for patent strategy review.

Narrow diagnostic promise:

Help the reader identify whether an AI-assisted invention workflow has enough non-confidential human-contribution documentation to support a more informed patent-strategy conversation.

Plain-language premise:

The useful question is not simply “Did we use AI?” It is “Can we explain the human technical contribution, when it happened, how it shaped the invention, and where the records live?”

Core educational outline:

1. Start with the human contribution, not the tool.
   - What technical problem did the human team identify?
   - What design choices, architecture decisions, experiments, or claim-worthy improvements came from people?
2. Separate AI assistance from conception records.
   - Prompts, model output, notes, prototypes, diagrams, and test results may be part of the evidence trail, but should not be treated as a legal conclusion.
3. Map ownership before disclosure.
   - Employee, contractor, founder, advisor, vendor, open-source, and AI-tool terms may all affect the ownership record.
4. Connect patent strategy to trade-secret and product-roadmap strategy.
   - Not every technical differentiator belongs in a patent filing; some may need trade-secret controls or contract support.
5. Choose one 10-day cleanup action.
   - Inventory invention notebooks, prompt/prototype records, assignment documents, vendor terms, and public-disclosure timing before a strategy review.

Suggested viewer/reader takeaway:

If the invention story cannot be told without confidential detail, use an internal non-public checklist first. The public diagnostic should only ask categorical questions about stage, documentation, ownership, disclosure timing, and AI-tool use.

## Brief 2 — AI Compliance

Working title: “AI Governance Readiness Without Overreacting: The Use-Case Inventory First”

Authority pillar: AI Legal Risk and Compliance

Primary audience:

- CEOs, COOs, CTOs, GCs, HR leaders, product leaders, and risk leaders adopting AI internally or embedding AI in products.
- Secondary: CFOs and operators who need an owner map before budget, vendor, or board conversations.

Narrow diagnostic promise:

Help the reader determine whether the organization has a basic AI use-case inventory with owners, data categories, vendor/model references, human review points, records, and escalation paths.

Plain-language premise:

A policy is hard to operationalize if the company has not first mapped where AI is used, what data is involved, who reviews outputs, and which records show the process.

Core educational outline:

1. Inventory before policy.
   - List AI use cases across product, engineering, sales, support, HR, finance, legal, and operations.
2. Classify data and decision exposure.
   - Use non-confidential categories: customer data, employee data, public data, sensitive data, regulated data, internal business data.
3. Identify vendors, models, and contract touchpoints.
   - Link each use case to vendor terms, data rights, confidentiality, security, retention, indemnity, audit, and output-use questions.
4. Add human review and records.
   - Who reviews AI-assisted outputs before customer, employee, financial, legal, or product decisions?
5. Assign governance ownership.
   - AI readiness usually needs an operating owner, escalation route, training path, and update cadence.
6. Choose one 10-day cleanup action.
   - Build a use-case inventory with owner, data category, vendor/model, human review, records location, and next review date.

Suggested viewer/reader takeaway:

AI readiness should not start with panic or a generic policy. It should start with a practical inventory that helps leadership see which use cases need attention first.

## Brief 4 — M&A Readiness

Working title: “Founder M&A Readiness: The Non-Financial Diligence Map”

Authority pillar: M&A, Diligence, and Transaction Readiness

Primary audience:

- Founders, CEOs, CFOs, COOs, board members, and GCs preparing for potential acquisition, investment, strategic partnership, or buyer diligence.
- Secondary: technology-company operators organizing records before banker, investor, acquirer, or board conversations.

Narrow diagnostic promise:

Help the reader identify non-financial diligence categories that should be mapped before a buyer, investor, or strategic partner asks for them.

Plain-language premise:

Deal readiness is not only a financial model. For technology companies, diligence often turns on whether ownership, contracts, records, employment/contractor materials, AI/data/privacy posture, and corporate approvals can be explained clearly.

Core educational outline:

1. Map the non-financial diligence categories.
   - Corporate records, cap table support, IP ownership, customer/vendor contracts, employment/contractor documents, privacy/security, AI/data, disputes, and concentration risk.
2. Separate “exists somewhere” from “ready to explain.”
   - A document may exist but still be stale, unsigned, inconsistent, hard to locate, or missing owner/context.
3. Focus on ownership and assignability.
   - Invention assignments, contractor/vendor rights, open-source records, customer change-of-control or assignment terms, and data-use rights may all matter.
4. Add AI/data/privacy diligence early.
   - Buyers may ask how AI, product data, customer data, vendor tools, records, and human review are governed.
5. Choose one 60-day cleanup plan.
   - Identify the three categories most likely to slow diligence and assign owners, evidence locations, and counsel-review questions.

Suggested viewer/reader takeaway:

The goal is not to predict a deal outcome. It is to reduce avoidable scramble by knowing which diligence categories are organized, which are incomplete, and which need legal review before a process starts.
