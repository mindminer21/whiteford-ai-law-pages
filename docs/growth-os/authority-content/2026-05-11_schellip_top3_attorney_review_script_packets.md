## Packet 1 — AI-assisted invention human-contribution records

### Final title

AI Helped Build It. What Should You Document Before a Patent Review?

Supporting SEO phrases:

- AI patent lawyer

Thumbnail hook:

AI helped. Who contributed?

### Audience and promise

Audience: founders, CTOs, R&D leaders, product leaders, and inventors using AI tools while building technical products.

Educational promise: help viewers organize a non-confidential human-contribution record before a patent-strategy conversation, without giving an inventorship, patentability, ownership, or eligibility opinion.

### 30-second cold open

If AI helped you build the invention, the first useful patent question is not simply, “Did you use AI?”

The better starting point is: can the human contribution be explained from the record?

Who framed the technical problem? Who selected the design path? Who tested the result? Who made the choices that turned an AI-assisted output into a working technical solution?

In this video, I’ll walk through the non-confidential records founders should organize before a patent review — not to decide inventorship on YouTube, but to make the first strategy conversation more focused, safer, and more useful.

### 8–12 minute script outline

0:00–0:45 — Cold open: AI use is not the whole question

- Say the issue is not simply whether a team used ChatGPT, a coding assistant, a model, an optimizer, or an AI design tool.
- Frame the practical question: what human contribution can be explained without exposing confidential details publicly?
- Source-current note: USPTO public materials emphasize the same inventorship standard regardless of AI use, human contribution to conception, and natural-person inventors.
- Guardrail: do not analyze any specific invention in public.

0:45–1:45 — Why the record matters before the legal analysis

- Explain that patent strategy usually depends on facts: who did what, when, and with what technical contribution.
- Distinguish a useful internal record from a public disclosure.
- Use safe examples of record categories: dated project notes, architecture decisions, test summaries, prototype milestones, problem statements, and contribution logs.
- Avoid telling viewers that any category proves inventorship or patentability.

1:45–3:00 — Separate tool use from contribution records

- AI can appear in many places: brainstorming, code generation, simulation, prior-art searching, test design, documentation, UI generation, or analytics.
- The public video should not ask viewers to reveal prompts, code, model details, trade secrets, or unpublished invention details.
- Suggested internal record categories:
  - problem framing
  - design alternatives considered
  - selected architecture or workflow
  - human edits or constraints
  - validation and testing choices
  - reasons rejected paths did not work

3:00–4:30 — Map human choices in the development timeline

- Encourage viewers to build a timeline with non-confidential labels:
  - problem identified
  - first prototype
  - AI tool used as assistant
  - human decision point
  - test or validation milestone
  - technical improvement identified
  - pre-disclosure review point
- Explain that counsel may later ask more specific questions through a protected channel after conflicts and engagement steps.

4:30–5:45 — Ownership context to flag early

- Discuss categories that should be flagged for counsel review:
  - employee versus contractor work
  - vendor or platform terms
  - university or accelerator involvement
  - open-source components
  - customer-funded development
  - side-project or prior-employer issues
- Guardrail: do not ask viewers to send contracts or proprietary details through a public form.

5:45–7:00 — Patent, trade secret, and disclosure timing

- Explain that patent strategy is often connected to disclosure timing: demos, fundraising, pilots, launch announcements, papers, GitHub, and sales decks.
- Use educational phrasing: “these are issues to organize before a strategy review,” not “you must file before X” or “your disclosure bars rights.”
- Include practical pre-review question: “What public disclosures are planned or already made?” without asking them to reveal the content publicly.

7:00–8:30 — What to organize before the first patent-strategy call

- Non-confidential checklist:
  - inventor/contributor roster by role
  - project timeline
  - AI tool categories used
  - human decision points
  - prototype/test milestones
  - public disclosure dates/categories
  - vendor/contract/open-source issue flags
  - current business goal: protect, disclose, fundraise, license, sell, or hold as trade secret
- Explain that the goal is to identify the next questions, not to self-diagnose legal conclusions.

8:30–9:30 — CTA: AI Patent Strategy Scorecard

- Educational only; not legal advice.

### 30-second cold open

If your company is trying to prepare for Colorado AI and automated-decision rules, do not start by copying a generic AI policy.

Start by finding the AI uses that already exist.

Which department owns them? Which vendor or internal tool is involved? What data goes in? What output comes out? Is a person reviewing the output before it affects someone? Where are the records?

Colorado timing is moving, and attorney review should confirm the latest status before public claims. But the practical first step is stable: build the inventory before you debate the policy.

0:00–0:45 — Cold open: inventory before policy

- Say generic policies fail when they do not match real use cases.

- Define inventory in plain terms: a map of where AI tools or automated decision-support tools are used in the business.
- Suggested columns:
  - department
  - business owner
  - vendor/tool
  - internal or external use
  - data category
  - output category
  - human reviewer
  - records location
  - current policy/contract owner

- Ask teams to identify whether outputs guide, assist, recommend, score, rank, classify, or predict something used in a person-affecting decision.

3:15–4:45 — Vendor and contract triage

- Inventory vendor/tool contracts without asking for details publicly.
- Counsel-review flags:
  - data rights
  - confidentiality
  - security
  - retention/deletion
  - audit/cooperation rights
  - model/output use
  - indemnity and limitation of liability
  - subcontractors or data transfers
- Emphasize that contracts should be reviewed in a protected channel.

4:45–6:00 — Human review and records

- Map where a human reviewer exists and what they are expected to do.
- Ask whether review is meaningful, documented, escalated, or just rubber-stamped.
- Record categories:
  - owner
  - review step
  - exception process
  - adverse-outcome escalation path
  - training or policy reference
  - retention location
- Guardrail: do not request employee/applicant/customer/patient-specific facts.

6:00–7:15 — Use NIST as vocabulary, not a Colorado checklist

- Mention NIST AI RMF as neutral vocabulary for govern, map, measure, and manage.
- Avoid presenting NIST as the legal compliance checklist for Colorado.
- Translate into operational questions:
  - Have we mapped the system?
  - Have we measured known risks or limitations?
  - Who manages updates and incidents?
  - Who governs vendor and business-owner accountability?

7:15–8:45 — 30/60/90 readiness plan

- 30 days: find use cases, owners, vendors, data categories, and human-review points.
- 60 days: triage high-priority systems and contract/policy gaps for counsel review.
- 90 days: build repeatable intake, review, records, vendor, and change-management workflows.
- Phrase as planning structure, not compliance guarantee.
